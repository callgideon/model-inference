### SXM vs PCIe

There is no PCIe B200. Every number in this document is **SXM6, 1000 W, NVLink-connected**. Do not
carry any of it across to a PCIe Blackwell part (RTX PRO 6000 Server Edition, 96 GB GDDR7, `sm_120`)
— different memory technology, different bandwidth class, different kernel support (`sm_120` is
explicitly *not* the same target as `sm_100`, see §5).

## 2. Memory and bandwidth

### The 180 GB vs 192 GB question — resolved

Both numbers circulate. They are not both wrong; they describe different things.

| Claim | Where it comes from | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **192 GB** | GTC-2024-era announcement material and a long tail of blog/vendor pages that copied it (e.g. [src](https://www.spheron.network/blog/nvidia-b200-complete-guide/), [src](https://jarvislabs.ai/ai-faqs/nvidia-b200-specs)); also repeated by the vLLM blog ("B200/GB200 GPUs with 192GB HBM") [src](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-02-01-gpt-oss-optimizations) | **Physical stack capacity**, 8 HBM3e stacks × 24 GB. Not what the shipping part exposes. |
| **180 GB** | Lenovo's ThinkSystem HGX B200 product guide for the actual orderable SKU [src](https://lenovopress.lenovo.com/lp2226-thinksystem-nvidia-b200-180gb-1000w-gpu); NVIDIA DGX B200 page, "1,440 GB total" for 8 GPUs [src](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/dgx-b200/); Oracle's shape catalogue, "8x Nvidia B200 180GB" [src](https://www.oracle.com/cloud/price-list/); CoreWeave's HGX B200 row, VRAM 180 [src](https://www.coreweave.com/pricing); Lambda, "B200 SXM6 180 GB VRAM" [src](https://lambda.ai/service/gpu-cloud); RunPod, "180 GB VRAM" [src](https://www.runpod.io/pricing); GCP `a4-highgpu-8g`, "GPU memory 1,440 GB HBM3e" [src](https://cloud.google.com/compute/docs/accelerator-optimized-machines); AWS `p6-b200.48xlarge`, 1,440 GiB [src](https://instances.vantage.sh/aws/ec2/p6-b200.48xlarge) | **Correct for every shipping HGX/DGX B200 and every cloud instance.** Plan with 180 GB. |

**Plan with 180 GB.** Anyone quoting 192 GB for an HGX B200 is quoting the 2024 announcement.

A second, smaller discrepancy: **GB vs GiB.** Cloud spec sheets (AWS, GCP) print "1,440 GiB" while
NVIDIA prints "1,440 GB". If it is decimal GB, 180 GB = 167.6 GiB; if binary, 180 GiB = 193.3 GB.
⚠️ **TO BE VERIFIED** against `nvidia-smi` on a real node. This document uses the **conservative**
reading — 180 × 10⁹ bytes — for every fit calculation.

⚠️ **TO BE VERIFIED — the 7.7 vs 8.0 TB/s discrepancy (flagged once, here).** Lenovo's orderable-SKU
page prints 7.7 TB/s per GPU; NVIDIA's DGX B200 page prints "1,440 GB total, 64 TB/s HBM3e bandwidth"

The gap is ~4 % and neither source is retracted. **This document plans with 7.7 TB/s** — it is the
number on the SKU sheet for the part you can buy, and being 4 % pessimistic is the right direction
for capacity planning. **Every roofline in §9 states which figure it used**; re-derive at 8.0 TB/s by
multiplying any decode throughput here by 1.039.

The 4.14 TB/s STREAM result is *not* the number to plan LLM decode with: STREAM Triad is a
read-read-write pattern with no reuse and poor access-pattern tuning for HBM3e, whereas a decode
step is a near-pure streaming read of weights with TMA-friendly layouts. Published serving
measurements imply materially higher MBU — see §9.

### Usable capacity (METHODOLOGY §3)

```
usable_hbm = 180 GB × 0.90 = 162 GB per GPU      (= 150.9 GiB)
           = 1,296 GB per 8-GPU HGX B200 node    (= 1,207.0 GiB)
```

(All arithmetic is done in bytes. The capacity columns in this document are decimal **GB** = 10⁹ B,
because that is the unit every vendor SKU sheet uses; the KV table below reports **GiB** = 2³⁰ B, per
METHODOLOGY §1.)

Subtract `activation_ws ≈ 2–6 GB/GPU` at 8K context (more with long chunked-prefill chunks, CUDA
graph pools, and NVFP4 autotuning workspaces — FlashInfer autotune in particular allocates
scratch during warm-up, see §11), then:

```
kv_budget_per_gpu = 162 GB − per_gpu_weights − activation_ws
```

Practical rule for B200: **~155 GB of genuinely allocatable HBM per GPU**, ~1,240 GB per node
(≈ 144 GiB / 1,155 GiB).

### KV cache and per-sequence state, the four models in this repo

Per METHODOLOGY §2 and the pinned per-model values in METHODOLOGY §8. `kv_bytes_per_token` and the
fixed per-sequence state are model properties, not B200 properties; they are reproduced here so the
180 GB budget above can be spent against something concrete. KV totals are **per sequence**, in
**GiB**, computed as `(ctx × kv_bytes_per_token + fixed_state) / 2³⁰`.

| Model (exp) | KV dtype / geometry | B/token | Fixed state / seq | 8K | 32K | 128K | 1M |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash** (`deepseek41f`) | FP4 main KV + indexer (**Blackwell-only kernel**, 720 + 170 B) | **890** | 2.77 MiB sliding-window ring (FP8; 5.38 MiB at BF16 ⚠️) | **0.009** | **0.030** | **0.111** | **0.872** |
| **Qwen3.8-27B** (`qwen3827b`) | BF16, 16 full-attention layers (48 linear-attention layers cache nothing) | **65,536** | 153.9 MB GDN state (fp32) | 0.643 | 2.143 | 8.143 | 64.143 |
| **Qwen3.8-27B** (`qwen3827b`) | FP8, same 16 layers | **32,768** | 78.4 MB GDN state (bf16) ⚠️ engine-dependent | 0.323 | 1.073 | 4.073 | 32.073 |
| **Kimi-K3** (`kimik3`) | FP8 MLA, 24 of 93 layers | **13,824** (13.5 KiB) | **2.25 GB** = 428.6 MiB × **S = 5** slots (SGLang) | 2.198 | 2.515 | 3.780 | 15.593 |
| **Marlin-2B** (`marlin2b`) | BF16, 6 GQA layers | **12,288** (12 KiB) | 18.63 MiB GDN | 0.112 | 0.393 | 1.518 | 12.018 |

Two things fall out of this table on a 180 GB part:

- **DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash's 890 B/token is the FP4-KV figure and it needs a Blackwell kernel** — the
  number does not transfer to Hopper or to `sm_120`. At 128K a sequence costs 0.111 GiB, so KV is
  never the binding constraint for this model; weights are.
- **Kimi-K3's cost is the recurrent state, not the cache.** The KDA state is multiplied by the
  engine's slot count (`S = 5` on SGLang), so **every request costs 2.25 GB before a single token is
  cached**. At 8K, 95 % of a Kimi-K3 sequence's memory is fixed state. Concurrency on Kimi-K3 is set
  by `S`, and dropping `S` is worth more than any KV quantization.

Per-(model, GPU) fit, throughput and cost tables are **not** in this document — they live in
`research/models/<exp>/b200.md` and are written in the next phase.

Notes that matter for planning:

- **FP6 buys memory, not FLOPs.** FP6 and FP8 share the 4,500 TFLOPS dense line. FP6 is a
  0.75 bytes/param format for bandwidth-bound decode; it is not a compute optimisation.
- **FP4 is exactly 2× FP8 dense.** 9 PFLOPS dense / 18 PFLOPS sparse. NVIDIA's DGX B200 "144
  petaFLOPS inference" headline is the **sparse** 8-GPU FP4 figure
  [src](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/dgx-b200/) — **marketing claim, sparse**. The dense
  node figure is 72 PFLOPS.
- **Structural sparsity is essentially unused in LLM serving.** No mainstream engine ships 2:4
  sparse LLM weights on Blackwell as of 2026-09-19. Treat every sparse column as unreachable.

Jarmusch & Chandrasekaran, *Microbenchmarking NVIDIA's Blackwell Architecture: An in-depth
Architectural Analysis* (arXiv 2512.02189, first posted 2025-12-01, rev. 2026-03-02 ⚠️ revision
dates **TO BE VERIFIED**), Table VI, single-instruction `tcgen05.mma` at m64n8k16
[src](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02189v3), [src](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02189):

| dtype | Measured | vs dense spec |
|---|---|---|
| FP4 | **3,850.1 TFLOPS** | 43 % of 9,000 |
| FP6 | **2,567.2 TFLOPS** | 57 % of 4,500 |
| FP8 | **1,912.8 TFLOPS** | 43 % of 4,500 |
| INT8 | **3,928.5 TOPS** | 87 % of 4,500 |
| FP16 | **964.8 TFLOPS** | 43 % of 2,250 |
| BF16 | **481.6 TFLOPS** | 21 % of 2,250 |
| TF32 | **482.4 TFLOPS** | 44 % of 1,100 |

The paper's Table VII reports 96–99 % of *its own* theoretical peak, i.e. the authors compute peak
from clock × instruction throughput at this tile shape, not from NVIDIA's datasheet. **These are
single-instruction-issue microbenchmarks at a tiny tile, not GEMM throughput** — they characterise
latency and issue rate, not achievable FLOPs. The anomalous BF16 = ½ FP16 result is an artifact of
that methodology and does not reflect NVIDIA's spec (both are 2,250 TFLOPS dense). Use them for
*relative* dtype ordering, not for sizing. Real GEMM numbers are in §6.

Also from that paper: `tcgen05.mma` single-instruction latency is **11.0–11.4 cycles** vs
`wgmma`'s 32–128 on Hopper (Table V); TMEM is most efficient at 64×64-element tiles (4 KB at FP8)
and underutilised below 32×32 (§V-A); the on-die decompression engine sustains **539 GB/s (ANS)**,
462 GB/s (Bitcomp), 213 GB/s (Cascaded), 155 GB/s (Zstd) output (Table I).

### Standard node topology

8× B200 SXM6 on one HGX baseboard, fully connected through **4 NVSwitch chips** — every GPU pair at
the full 1.8 TB/s, no oversubscription inside the node. Two x86 sockets (Intel Xeon Platinum 8570 on
DGX B200), PCIe Gen5 to the NICs and NVMe.

### The multi-node consideration that decides your architecture

**HGX B200's NVLink domain stops at 8 GPUs.** This is the single most important topological fact
about the part, and the one that separates it from GB200/GB300 NVL72:

- TP ≤ 8 is free-ish: NVLink at 1.8 TB/s/GPU, expect ~5–15 % comm overhead (METHODOLOGY §4).
- **TP > 8 crosses the 400 Gb/s-per-GPU InfiniBand/Ethernet fabric — a 36× bandwidth cliff**
  (1.8 TB/s → 50 GB/s per GPU). METHODOLOGY's 20–40 % multi-node TP overhead applies, and for
  latency-sensitive decode it is usually worse than that.
- Consequence: **wide-EP MoE serving (DeepEP/EPLB across 32–72 experts-worth of GPUs) is a GB200
  NVL72 play, not an HGX B200 play.** On B200 you keep the expert-parallel group inside one node
  (EP ≤ 8) and scale out with data parallelism / replicas, or you accept the fabric hit.
- NVIDIA's own InferenceMAX write-up leans on this: it attributes the gpt-oss-120b DEP gains
  specifically to "the 1,800 GB/s bidirectional bandwidth of NVLink and the NVLink Switch, which
  avoids traditional PCIe bottlenecks" [src](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-blackwell-leads-on-new-semianalysis-inferencemax-benchmarks/) — all of which is *intra-node*.
- Disaggregated prefill/decode (Dynamo, SGLang PD) is the right multi-node pattern here: it moves
  KV blocks over the fabric rather than running collectives across it. SGLang v0.5.20 verified
  DSpark under PD with decode context parallelism "on 8× B300 over NIXL and Mooncake up to 256K
  input" [src](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases) — the same code path applies to B200
  but the published verification is on B300. ⚠️ **TO BE VERIFIED** on B200.

### FP8 / FP4 attention and paged KV

### NVFP4 format, precisely

NVIDIA's own definition [src](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/introducing-nvfp4-for-efficient-and-accurate-low-precision-inference/) (published 2025-06-24):

- **Element:** E2M1 — 1 sign, 2 exponent, 1 mantissa bit. Representable magnitudes 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5,
  2, 3, 4, 6 (± ), range ≈ −6…+6.
- **Micro-block:** **16 values**, each block carrying **one shared FP8 E4M3 scale** — half MXFP4's
  32-value block, which is where the accuracy edge comes from.
- **Tensor level:** one FP32 (E8M23) scalar per tensor.
- **Hardware:** *"NVIDIA Blackwell fifth-generation Tensor Core architecture implements NVFP4"* —
  Blackwell and Blackwell Ultra. **Not Hopper.**
- **Accuracy:** on DeepSeek-R1-0528, **≤1 % degradation vs FP8** across benchmarks, with AIME 2024
  showing a 2 % *improvement* in NVFP4. No BF16 comparison given. **No MXFP4 head-to-head accuracy
  numbers are published in that post** — the 16-vs-32 block argument is structural, not measured.
- **Memory:** ~3.5× reduction vs FP16, 1.8× vs FP8.
- **Energy:** "25× for Blackwell and 50× for Blackwell Ultra versus H100" — **marketing claim**,
  not a like-for-like measurement.

Storage cost per METHODOLOGY §1: NVFP4 = 0.5 B/param + 1 FP8 E4M3 scale per 16 elements
(= 0.0625 B/param, **+12.5 % over the raw 4 bits**) → **0.5625 B/param** (4.5 bits). MXFP4 = 0.5 +
1 E8M0 per 32 elements (= 0.03125 B/param, **+6.25 %**) → **0.53125 B/param** (4.25 bits). MXFP4 is
marginally smaller; NVFP4 is more accurate. On B200 both run at 9,000 TFLOPS dense.

> **Corrected 2026-09-19.** This paragraph previously read "+6.25 % → 0.53125" for NVFP4 and
> "+3.1 % → 0.5156" for MXFP4, i.e. it multiplied 0.5 by the overhead instead of adding the scale
> bytes, and it inherited METHODOLOGY §1's "+6.25 % / +3.1 %" percentages, which are the overheads
> expressed against a *different* base. The bit counts are unambiguous: NVFP4 is 4 + 8/16 = 4.5
> bits/param, MXFP4 is 4 + 8/32 = 4.25 bits/param. The old NVFP4 figure understated weight memory
> by **5.9 %** — on a 671 B model that is 21 GB. ⚠️ METHODOLOGY §1's percentage wording should be
> fixed to match.

**Which FP4 does DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash actually ship?** **MXFP4** (E2M1 + one E8M0 per 32) for the
routed experts, with the non-expert tensors at FP8 E4M3 on a 32×32 UE8M0 tile scale — **not NVFP4**.
The checkpoint totals **510.29 GB**. NVIDIA's separate NVFP4 build (`deepseek41fnvfp4`) is
**527.27 GB — larger, not smaller**, because only ~58 % of its bytes are NVFP4 (routed experts,
group size 16) while the Engram tables stay FP8; it is accuracy-neutral and **no speedup over the
base checkpoint has been published** ⚠️. On B200 both formats run on the same 9,000 TFLOPS dense FP4
line, so there is no tensor-core reason to prefer the NVFP4 build
[METHODOLOGY §8](../METHODOLOGY.md), [models/deepseek41fnvfp4/architecture.md](../models/deepseek41fnvfp4/architecture.md).
Where SGLang's release notes below say "DeepSeek-V4-Flash NVFP4", that is SGLang's own label for the
NVFP4 build, not a statement about the base checkpoint's expert format.

### Measured NVFP4 MoE GEMM throughput on B200 — the number that should temper expectations

Independent benchmark on B200 (`sm_100a`), published 2026-01-05
[src](https://huggingface.co/blog/apsys/blackwell-nvfp4-comparison), `meas.`:

**gpt-oss-20B shape (32 experts, top-4), batch 4096:**

| Implementation | TFLOPS | vs BF16 | % of 9,000 dense FP4 peak |
|---|---|---|---|
| SGLang | **1,262** | 3.54× | 14.0 % |
| FlashInfer CuteDSL | **1,225** | 3.43× | 13.6 % |
| vLLM | **1,117** | 3.24× | 12.4 % |

**DeepSeek-scaled shape (256 experts, top-8), batch 4096:**

| Implementation | TFLOPS | vs BF16 | % of peak |
|---|---|---|---|
| FlashInfer | **1,132** | 4.69× | 12.6 % |
| SGLang | **993** | 4.12× | 11.0 % |
| vLLM | **968** | 4.08× | 10.8 % |

At batch 1 (interactive), per-layer latency: SGLang FP4 206.9 µs, vLLM FP4 369.5 µs, FlashInfer FP4
481.9 µs — **1.78× SGLang-to-vLLM and 2.33× SGLang-to-FlashInfer, on the same hardware and format**.
(The blog's own "1.78× faster" headline is the SGLang-vs-vLLM pair, not the full spread.)

**Read this carefully.** Grouped MoE GEMMs on B200 reach **11–14 % of the 9 PFLOPS dense FP4 peak**.
The 9 PFLOPS number is real silicon, but MoE routing, small per-expert tiles, and scale handling
leave most of it on the floor. Dense GEMMs do much better; the FlashMLA attention kernels do much
better (65 % MFU, §5). **Never size a MoE deployment off peak FP4.** METHODOLOGY's 0.2–0.35 MFU band
for FP4 is, for grouped-MoE shapes specifically, still optimistic — use 0.12–0.15 for the MoE GEMM
portion and the higher band for attention and dense layers.

## 9. Roofline numbers for inference

### 9.1 Bytes-per-FLOP and the ridge point

Using **HBM = 7.7 TB/s** and dense peaks from §3. `ridge = peak_FLOPS / bandwidth` is the arithmetic
intensity above which a kernel becomes compute-bound.

| dtype | Dense TFLOP/s | bytes/FLOP | **Ridge (FLOP/byte)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| FP64 | 37 | 0.2081 | 4.8 |
| FP32 | 75 | 0.1027 | 9.7 |
| TF32 | 1,100 | 0.00700 | 142.9 |
| BF16 / FP16 | 2,250 | 0.003422 | **292** |
| FP8 / FP6 / INT8 | 4,500 | 0.001711 | **584** |
| NVFP4 / MXFP4 | 9,000 | 0.000856 | **1,169** |

A decode step with batch *B* on a dense model has arithmetic intensity ≈ *B* (2 FLOP per weight
byte × B tokens ÷ 1 byte read, roughly). So on B200:

- **BF16 decode is bandwidth-bound below batch ~292.**
- **FP8 decode is bandwidth-bound below batch ~584.**
- **NVFP4 decode is bandwidth-bound below batch ~1,169.**

This is the central B200 planning fact: **the FP4 tensor cores double compute but the HBM did not
double, so the ridge point moved out to batch ~1,169.** Every realistic interactive serving point
(TPOT ≤ 50 ms) sits far below that. **On B200, NVFP4's value for decode is almost entirely the
halved weight bytes, not the doubled FLOPs.** The FLOPs pay off in prefill.

Compare H200: 4.8 TB/s, FP8 dense 1,979 TFLOPS [src](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/h200/)
⇒ ridge 412 FLOP/byte. B200's FP8 ridge (584) is 1.4× further out — B200 is *relatively* more
bandwidth-starved than H200 despite having 1.6× the bandwidth.

### 9.2 Decode upper bound

```
decode_step_time = bytes_per_decode_step / (7.7e12 × MBU)
tokens_per_s     = batch / decode_step_time
```

Worked examples at **MBU = 0.65** (METHODOLOGY's 0.5–0.7 band for first-gen Blackwell software,
upper half — the published measurements in §9.4 support it), 8K context, **using the 7.7 TB/s
planning peak** (§2; at 8.0 TB/s every tok/s below rises by 3.9 %):

| Model / precision | Parallelism | Weight bytes read/step | KV bytes read/step | Total | Step | Agg tok/s | **tok/s/GPU** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Llama-3.3-70B FP8, batch 32 | TP1 | 70.6 GB | **42.9 GB** | 113.5 GB | 22.69 ms | 1,410 | **1,410** |
| Llama-3.3-70B NVFP4, batch 32 | TP1 | **39.4 GB** | **42.9 GB** | 82.3 GB | 16.45 ms | 1,945 | **1,945** |
| gpt-oss-120b MXFP4, batch 64, all experts touched | TP1 | 61.0 GB | **9.8 GB** | 70.8 GB | 14.15 ms | 4,523 | **4,523** |
| DeepSeek-R1 671B NVFP4, batch 64 | TP8 | **377.4 GB** (node) | **18.4 GB** | 395.9 GB | 9.89 ms | 6,473 | **809** |

> **Corrected 2026-09-19.** The KV column previously read 0.5 / 0.5 / 0.5 / 0.3 GB, which is not
> what METHODOLOGY §4 specifies: a decode step re-reads the *whole* cache, so
> `kv_read = batch × ctx × kv_bytes_per_token`. At batch 32 and 8K that is
> `32 × 8192 × 163,840 B = 42.9 GB` for Llama-3.3-70B, ~86× the old figure, and it dominates the
> FP8 row. §9.4 of this same document already does this arithmetic correctly
> (`200 seq × 1K ctx × 160 KiB = 33.6 GB`), so the two sections contradicted each other. Weight
> bytes for the NVFP4/MXFP4 rows were also recomputed at the corrected **0.5625 B/param** (§6):
> 70 B → 39.4 GB, 671 B → 377.4 GB. Throughput falls by 1.2–2.2× across the table.

The gpt-oss-120b KV figure uses **36 layers, 8 KV heads, head_dim 64, and 18 of the 36 layers
sliding-window with W = 128** — the geometry [METHODOLOGY §5](../METHODOLOGY.md) pins for this model,
with the sliding-window layers capped at W tokens rather than at ctx:
`64 × 8192 × 18 × 2 × 8 × 64 + 64 × 128 × 18 × 2 × 8 × 64 = 9.8 GB` at FP8 KV. (An earlier revision
marked this ⚠️ **TO BE VERIFIED** for want of a checked-in `config.json`; METHODOLOGY §5 now fixes it.
For reference, if all 36 layers were full attention the KV term would be 19.3 GB and the row would
read ~3,650 tok/s.)

`est.` — arithmetic is `bytes / (n_gpus × 7.7e12 × 0.65)` (the 7.7 TB/s planning peak, §2). KV per
token: Llama-3.3-70B GQA (80 layers × 2 × 8 kv-heads × 128 head-dim × 1 B FP8) =
**163,840 B = 160 KiB/token**; DeepSeek-R1/V3 MLA (61 layers × 576 elements) =
**70,272 / 35,136 / 17,568 B per token at BF16 / FP8 / FP4** — the FP8 figure, 34.3 KiB, is the one
used above, and MLA is **4.66× cheaper per token** than the Llama GQA layout, which is why
DeepSeek-class models tolerate huge concurrency on a 180 GB part.

**Feasibility check** (METHODOLOGY §3 consistency rule) — every batch in the table above is below
`max_concurrency(ctx)` at that GPU count, so no row is `infeasible (KV)`. At 8K with
`activation_ws = 4 GB`: Llama-3.3-70B FP8 TP1 has an 87.4 GB KV budget ÷ 1.342 GB/seq ⇒ **65**;
NVFP4 TP1 ⇒ **88**; gpt-oss-120b TP1 ⇒ **633**; DeepSeek-R1 NVFP4 TP8 has 886 GB of node KV budget
÷ 0.288 GB/seq ⇒ **3,080**. Batches of 32 / 32 / 64 / 64 all fit.

The MoE weight-read term uses METHODOLOGY's
`distinct_experts(batch) ≈ n_experts × (1 − (1 − top_k/n_experts)^batch)`; at batch ≥ 64 with
top-8-of-256 routing this saturates to essentially all experts, so "read all weights" is the right
approximation above batch ~64 and the table uses it.

### 9.3 Prefill upper bound

```
prefill_tok_s = peak_FLOPS(dtype) × MFU / (2 × active_params)
```

| Model | Active params | dtype | MFU assumed | **prefill tok/s/GPU** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Llama-3.3-70B | 70 B | FP8 | 0.35 | **11,250** |
| Llama-3.3-70B | 70 B | NVFP4 | 0.30 | **19,286** |
| DeepSeek-R1 | 37 B | FP8 | 0.30 | **18,243** |
| DeepSeek-R1 | 37 B | NVFP4 | 0.25 | **30,405** |
| gpt-oss-120b | 5.1 B | MXFP4 | 0.20 | **176,471** |
| DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash | 8 B (prefill) | **MXFP4** (routed experts; non-expert tensors FP8) | 0.22 | **123,750** |

`est.` Attention FLOPs are *not* included (METHODOLOGY §4 adds
`4 × n_q_heads × head_dim × T²` per full-attention layer); at 8K they add 10–25 % for dense
attention and far less for DSA/CSA sparse models. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash activates **8 B params per
token during prefill and 16 B during decode** — its CED architecture makes it dramatically cheaper
to prefill than to decode, inverting the usual ratio
[src](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash). Its routed experts are **MXFP4, not
NVFP4** (§6); on B200 the two share the 9,000 TFLOPS dense FP4 line, so the row is unchanged by the
correction.

For the MoE cases, remember §6: measured grouped-MoE FP4 GEMMs hit only **11–14 %** of peak. The
0.20–0.25 MFU figures above are therefore *optimistic for MoE* and should be read as an upper bound
that assumes the attention and dense portions pull the average up.

### 9.4 Observed MBU / MFU from published benchmarks

**Llama 3.3 70B NVFP4 on B200**, NVIDIA/InferenceMAX v1: *"10,000 tokens per second"* per GPU at
**50 TPS/user** [src](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-blackwell-leads-on-new-semianalysis-inferencemax-benchmarks/).
Derived:

```python
batch = 10000 / 50 = 200 concurrent per GPU
step  = 1/50 = 20 ms
weights (NVFP4 70B, 0.5625 B/param)    = 39.4 GB     # corrected from 37.5 GB, see §6
KV (FP8, 160 KiB/tok, 1K ctx, 200 seq) = 33.6 GB
total = 72.9 GB / 0.020 s = 3.65 TB/s  ->  MBU = 47.4 %  (against the 7.7 TB/s planning peak, §2)
                                            MBU = 45.6 %  (against NVIDIA's implied 8.0 TB/s)
compute = 2 * 70e9 * 200 / 0.020 = 1,400 TFLOP/s  ->  MFU (vs 9 PF NVFP4) = 15.6 %
```

**MBU ≈ 46–47 %, MFU ≈ 16 %** — a clean, decisively bandwidth-bound decode point, and a real
measurement to anchor planning on. It sits below METHODOLOGY's 0.5–0.7 Blackwell band; 0.5 is the
right planning default for Llama-class dense decode on B200 today, with 0.65 as a stretch target.

**gpt-oss-120b on B200**, TensorRT-LLM, same source:

| Point | Value | Date | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Max throughput | **60,000 TPS/GPU** | TRT-LLM of 2025-10-09 | Very high concurrency, beyond InferenceMAX's 4–64 sweep |
| At 100 TPS/user | **30,000 TPS/GPU** | 2025-10-09 | 5× the 2025-08-05 launch-day ~6K |
| At 100 TPS/user with EAGLE-3 spec decode | 10K → **30,000 TPS/GPU** | — | 3× from `gpt-oss-120b-Eagle3-v2` |
| Max interactivity stated by the source | **400 TPS/user** (at $0.12/1M tokens) | 2025-10-09 | Re-fetched 2026-09-19: 400 TPS/user is the highest interactivity point the post states. A "1,000 TPS/user" row previously stood here and is **deleted — it is not in the cited source** |

Cross-check: 60,000 tok/s/GPU on a 5.1 B-active model is
`2 × 5.1e9 × 60,000 = 612 TFLOP/s` = 6.8 % MFU against 9 PFLOPS FP4, and requires reading ~61 GB of
MXFP4 weights per step at batch ≈ 60,000/(1/step). Both are physically consistent. **The number is
plausible, and it is a max-throughput, not an interactive, operating point.**

## 11. Known gotchas

1. **180 GB, not 192 GB.** Sizing a model to 192 GB/GPU and discovering 180 at deploy time is a
   6.25 % capacity error that lands exactly where it hurts — the KV budget. See §2.
2. **GB vs GiB is still unresolved.** Plan with 180 × 10⁹ bytes; if `nvidia-smi` shows ~183 GiB you
   get a free 8 % margin, and if it shows ~167 GiB you were right. ⚠️ **TO BE VERIFIED.**
3. **CUDA 12.8 is the floor, 12.9 is the real floor.** Toolkit 12.8 is the first that emits
   `compute_100` cubins [src](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/blackwell-compatibility-guide/index.html),
   but **FlashMLA's SM100 kernels require CUDA 12.9+** [src](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/FlashMLA)
   and vLLM ships CUDA 12.9 binaries by default
   [src](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/getting_started/installation/gpu.html). Standardise on 12.9
   or 13.x.
4. **`sm_100a` vs `sm_100` vs `sm_100f`.** CUTLASS and every tcgen05-accelerated kernel need
   `sm_100a`, which is **not forward compatible**. A wheel built `-arch=sm_100` will load and run
   *slowly* (or fail on arch-accelerated intrinsics). `sm_100f` (CUDA 12.9+) is the family target for
   one-binary-fits-10.x [src](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/blackwell-compatibility-guide/index.html).
   **Check what your wheel was built with before blaming the GPU.**
5. **`sm_100` ≠ `sm_120`.** Consumer/workstation Blackwell is a different support surface: FA-3 does
   not target SM120, TRT-LLM aborts on NVFP4 W4A16 MoE on SM120, and FlashInfer needed a separate
   issue (#5290) to unlock FP8 KV for DSA models there. B200 benchmarks do not transfer to RTX PRO
   6000, and vice versa.
6. **FlashMLA has no dense MLA decode kernel on SM100** (§5). Serving DeepSeek-V3/V3.1 on B200 means
   `trtllm_mla` or FlashInfer, not FlashMLA. Silently falling back to a Triton path will cost you a
   large multiple.
7. **FlashAttention-4 is beta.** 31 beta tags, no published SM-support matrix, an open maintainer
   question about which SMs are supported
   [src](https://github.com/Dao-AILab/flash-attention/issues/2376). Pin an exact tag; revalidate
   accuracy on every bump.
8. **FlashInfer autotune has crashed on SM100 NVFP4 MoE.** The 26.08 container re-enabled autotune
   after a cycle with it disabled
   [src](https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/frameworks/vllm-release-notes/rel-26-08.html). If you
   see MoE garbage output or warm-up aborts, force `--moe-backend flashinfer_cutlass`.
9. **Engine-version variance dwarfs hardware variance.** SGLang 0.5.5 → 0.5.6 moved B200
   DeepSeek-R1 by up to 1.79× [src](https://inferencex.semianalysis.com/blog/sglang-0-5-6-b200-deepseek-r1-fp4-up-to-1-8x);
   TRT-LLM moved gpt-oss-120b 5× in two months
   [src](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-blackwell-leads-on-new-semianalysis-inferencemax-benchmarks/);
   the three engines differ by **1.78× (SGLang 206.9 µs vs vLLM 369.5 µs) and 2.33× end to end
   (vs FlashInfer 481.9 µs)** on the *same* FP4 MoE kernel at batch 1
   [src](https://huggingface.co/blog/apsys/blackwell-nvfp4-comparison). **Any B200 benchmark without
   an exact engine version is worthless.**
10. **Power: 1,000 W per GPU, ~14.3 kW per node.** This is roughly 2× a DGX H100 rack slot. Air
    cooling an HGX B200 is possible but many colos will cap you; liquid is common. Under sustained
    NVFP4 MoE load the GPUs sit at TGP, so nameplate is the planning number, not a peak.
    ⚠️ **TO BE VERIFIED**: no public measurement of B200 clock-throttling behaviour under sustained
    FP4 load was located. Budget for it.
11. **Memory fragmentation with NVFP4 + long context.** NVFP4 weights, FP8/FP4 paged KV, CUDA-graph
    pools and autotuner workspaces all coexist; the 10 % haircut in METHODOLOGY §3 is not
    conservative here. Watch for OOM on the *second* long request, not the first.
12. **Structural sparsity is a paper feature.** Every "18 PFLOPS" / "144 PFLOPS" headline is 2:4
    sparse. No serving stack uses it. Halve it.
13. **FP6 is not a speed feature.** Same 4,500 TFLOPS dense line as FP8 (§3). If a vendor pitches
    "FP6 acceleration on Blackwell", they mean memory.
14. **Cloud quota reality.**
    - **Azure does not sell HGX B200** — its Blackwell SKUs are GB200/GB300 4-GPU VMs
      [src](https://prices.azure.com/api/retail/prices). An 8-GPU-NVLink assumption breaks there.
    - **GCP `a4-highgpu-8g` is reservation / Spot / Flex-start only** and is sold only as the whole
      8-GPU shape [src](https://www.thundercompute.com/blog/nvidia-b200-pricing) ⚠️.
    - **Crusoe lists B200 as "contact sales"** with no public price, while publishing H200 and H100
      rates [src](https://www.crusoe.ai/cloud/pricing) — a reasonable proxy for how tight B200
      allocation still is.
    - Multiple sources describe B200 in 2026 as **allocation-constrained rather than
      price-constrained** [src](https://www.mercatus-ai.com/blog/b200-server-price). Budget lead
      time, not just dollars.
15. **MIG carves B200 into 7 × 23 GB** [src](https://lenovopress.lenovo.com/lp2226-thinksystem-nvidia-b200-180gb-1000w-gpu).
    23 GB is enough for an 8B NVFP4 model and little else; MIG also kills NVLink collectives. Rarely
    the right answer for LLM serving on this part.
16. **Non-portable cluster size 16.** B200 allows it by opt-in but it is explicitly non-portable
    [src](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/blackwell-tuning-guide/index.html) — a kernel tuned this way
    will not run on other 10.x parts.

## 12. Comparison hooks

### (a) Decode bandwidth

**vs H100 (80 GB, 3.35 TB/s ⚠️ — H100 figures not fetched in this session, from the H200 page's
"H200 delivers nearly double the capacity with 1.4× more memory bandwidth" relative to H100
[src](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/h200/)):** B200's 7.7 TB/s is roughly **2.3× H100's**
and its 180 GB is **2.25× H100's 80 GB**. The two scale together, so a model that fits on *n* H100s
fits on about *n*/2.25 B200s and decodes about 2.3× faster per GPU at the same batch — but on
**half to a quarter as many GPUs**, so aggregate node decode throughput per 8-GPU node is ~2.3×, not
5×. Add NVFP4 halving the weight bytes again (a Hopper-impossible trick, §6) and the realistic dense
decode gain is **3–4×** — precisely the "3–4× higher performance than the best 8×H200 results" both
MLPerf v5.1 B200 submitters reported
[src](https://lambda.ai/blog/lambda-mlperf-inference-v5.1). Versus **H200** (141 GB, 4.8 TB/s
[src](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/h200/)) the raw bandwidth ratio is only **1.60×** and
capacity **1.28×**; the independent microbenchmark measured **1.27×** on FP16/FP8 tensor cores
(Table VII) and **1.58×** on an actual Mistral-7B FP8 inference run (Table VIII)
[src](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02189v3). The same paper's abstract reports **1.85× ResNet-50 and
1.55× GPT-1.3B mixed-precision training throughput, and 32 % better energy efficiency, vs H200**
[src](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02189). **Without FP4, B200 is a ~1.3–1.6× H200 — tracking the
1.60× bandwidth ratio, as a bandwidth-bound part should. With FP4, it is a 3–4×.** That is the whole
argument for the part.

**vs B200 (self):** n/a — this is the B200 document. For the B300/Blackwell-Ultra contrast:
B300 carries **268 GB/GPU as deployed** (2,144 GB/node; METHODOLOGY §8) against B200's 180 GB
(1,440 GB/node) — **1.49× the KV budget** — plus 2× attention performance and 2× the node scale-out
fabric (1.6 vs 0.8 TB/s)
[src](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/hgx/). For long-context and reasoning workloads B300
is the materially better part; B200 wins on FP64 (37 vs ~1.25 TFLOPS/GPU) and INT8 (72 vs 3 POPS
sparse per node), neither of which matters for LLM inference.

### (b) FP8 / FP4 prefill

**vs H100/H200:** H200 SXM peaks at **3,958 TFLOPS FP8 *with sparsity*** — **1,979 TFLOPS dense**
[src](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/h200/). B200 is **4,500 TFLOPS dense FP8**, a
**2.27× dense-to-dense** gain, and **9,000 TFLOPS dense NVFP4** — a format **Hopper cannot execute at
all**, giving **4.55× H200's dense FP8 prefill ceiling**. This is where Blackwell's compute actually
lands: prefill is compute-bound, so the FP4 tensor cores pay off directly, whereas in decode they sit
idle below batch ~1,169 (§9.1). Practically: NVIDIA reports >4× per-GPU throughput vs H200 on
Llama 3.3 70B at 50 TPS/user
[src](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-blackwell-leads-on-new-semianalysis-inferencemax-benchmarks/),
and FlashMLA's sparse MLA prefill runs **2.27× faster on B200 than H800**
[src](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/FlashMLA). Caveat from §6: grouped-MoE FP4 GEMMs reach only
**11–14 % of peak**, so the 4.55× ceiling is not what you measure on a MoE — expect 2.5–3.5×.

**vs B300:** **corrected 2026-09-19 — they are *not* identical on FP4.** NVIDIA's HGX page prints
FP4 Tensor Core as "144 PFLOPS | 72 PFLOPS" for HGX B200 and "144 PFLOPS | **108 PFLOPS**" for
HGX B300, under the footnote *"Specification: Sparse | Dense"*
[src](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/hgx/). The **sparse** headline is the same 144
PFLOPS, which is what makes the two look equal in marketing material, but **dense FP4 on B300 is
108 PFLOPS/node = 13.5 PFLOPS/GPU, 1.5× B200's 9 PFLOPS/GPU.** FP8/FP6 *is* identical (72 PFLOPS
sparse per node on both), as are FP16/BF16 (36 PFLOPS) and TF32 (18 PFLOPS). So B300's advantage is
memory, attention (2× "attention performance"), 2× scale-out fabric **and 1.5× dense FP4 GEMM** —
while B200 keeps FP64 (296 vs 10 TFLOPS/node) and INT8 (72 vs 3 POPS/node). If your workload is
prefill-FLOP-bound in FP4, **B300 prefills ~1.5× faster, not identically**; B200 is cheaper
($14.00 vs $15.00/GPU-hr on OCI; $7.15 vs $7.85 on Nebius
[src](https://apexapps.oracle.com/pls/apex/cetools/api/v1/products/?limit=1000&currencyCode=USD),
[src](https://nebius.com/prices)).

you can actually get an allocation for.** B200's case is availability, a mature 18-month software
stack, and spot pricing down to $3.95–4.26/GPU-hr that B300 does not yet have.
